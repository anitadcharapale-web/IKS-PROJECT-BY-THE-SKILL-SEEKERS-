Gunas and the Big Five

सत्त्व Sattva रजस् Rajas तमस् Tamas

India's three-strand model of personality and the Big Five, the model most psychologists use today, both try to describe how people differ. This page explains each, shows how far researchers have compared them, and sets out one comparison that still looks open: measuring both in the same people, in India and in another country.

Updated 18 November 2026

Where the research stands

Three short answers, from what is settled to what is open.

Well studied

Gunas have been measured in Indian samples for decades and linked to well-being, stress and behaviour at work. The Big Five has been tested in dozens of countries.

Partly studied

A 2026 study of 434 Indian young adults reports links between Gunas and the Big Five. A 2016 study compared Gunas in India, the USA and the Czech Republic, but looked at well-being, not the Big Five.

Open question

In this search, no study measured Gunas and the Big Five in the same people in India and in another country. That is the gap this project proposes to fill.

What are the Gunas?

In Samkhya philosophy, and later in the Bhagavad Gita, everything in nature is made of three qualities called gunas. The mind is part of nature, so the mind is made of them too. The Samkhyakarika, the main Samkhya text, compares them to three strands twisted into one rope.

Nobody is only one guna. Everyone carries all three, in different amounts. The mix shifts with habits, food, company and daily choices. The Gita describes this in Chapter 14: sometimes Sattva wins over the other two, sometimes Rajas, sometimes Tamas.

The aim is not to remove Rajas and Tamas. The source paper says the goal is to raise Sattva as the main orientation while using Rajas and Tamas where each one fits. The Gita's highest ideal, Gunatita, is to watch the three gunas without being ruled by any of them.

Researchers measure the gunas with questionnaires such as the Rao Triguna Scale and the Vedic Personality Inventory (VPI). Each person gets three scores, one per guna. Gunas have also been profiled in clinical groups, for example in a study that compared 113 patients with 113 matched healthy people (Bhargav et al., 2023).

The Big Five

The Big Five is the standard personality model in modern psychology research. It describes a person on five scales, often remembered as OCEAN. Each person gets one score per scale.

Where the two models might meet

The source paper proposes a rough match: Sattva with Agreeableness and Conscientiousness, Rajas with Extraversion and low Agreeableness, and Tamas with Neuroticism and low Conscientiousness. Why India needs a careful comparison

Before comparing Gunas across countries, note that even the Big Five behaves differently in some Indian samples. Three studies show how.

Big Five scores of Indian college students

T-scores from the 56-nation study. The U.S. average is set to 50, and one standard deviation is 10 points. The shaded band is half a standard deviation either side of the U.S. average.

India sample: 100 men and 100 women, English-language questionnaire. Source: Schmitt et al. (2007), Table 5. All five scores sit within half a standard deviation of the U.S. average, so the differences are small.

56 nations, one yardstick

The study used the 44-item Big Five Inventory in 56 nations. The five-factor structure held across world regions, though South and Southeast Asia, which includes India, was among the regions where it matched less closely. The paper reports lower extraversion there than in the rest of the world, and low reliability for Agreeableness in that region (.57).

Schmitt et al. (2007)

Five factors did not appear

400 adults from Udupi and Mangalore in Karnataka completed a short Big Five questionnaire. Factor analysis found four- and five-factor solutions, but none of the factors matched the original five. The authors question the cross-cultural validity of the Big Five and ask for culturally adapted measures.

Shetty, Thomas and Munoli (2023)

India and Japan

191 Indian and 248 Japanese university students completed a 10-item Big Five measure. Indian students scored higher on agreeableness, conscientiousness and openness. Japanese students scored higher on neuroticism. Extraversion did not differ. The Gunas were not measured.

Nishaat (2024)

The lesson for a new study: check each scale's reliability in every group before comparing scores across countries. A Guna measure gives a second view of the same people, which is one reason to measure both.

A study that would fill the gap

A survey of students in India and in one other country, using one Guna scale and one Big Five scale.

Research questions

  • RQ1In each country, how do Sattva, Rajas and Tamas scores relate to the five Big Five scores?
  • RQ2Is the pattern the same in India and in the comparison country?
  • RQ3Extension: do Guna scores relate to perceived stress or procrastination, and does that still hold after accounting for the Big Five?

Hypotheses

  • H1Sattva correlates positively with Conscientiousness and Agreeableness in both countries.
  • H2Tamas correlates positively with Neuroticism and negatively with Conscientiousness.
  • H3Exploratory. Sources differ on Rajas (Extraversion or Neuroticism), so test both without a prediction.
  • H4Exploratory. The strength of these links may differ between the two countries. The source paper says this is unresolved.

H1 and H2 come from the paper's proposed match and the 2026 Indian study.

  1. Choose the measures

    One Guna scale (for example the VPI, which the 2016 and 2026 studies above both used) and one Big Five scale (for example the 44-item Big Five Inventory). Check each scale's permission terms first. Some are free for research and some are sold commercially.

  2. Pick the groups

    College students in India and in one other country, in the same age range. Use the same language, or a properly translated version.

  3. Collect responses

    Use an online form with a short consent note, no names, and anonymous results. Ask your teacher whether college approval is needed.

  4. Check the scales

    Calculate Cronbach's alpha for every scale in each group. It shows whether a scale's items hang together, and values below about .70 call for care.

  5. Analyse

    Correlate each guna with each Big Five scale inside each group. That is 3 × 5 = 15 correlations per group. Then compare the two groups' patterns.

  6. Report

    Show the results in a table like the one in the overlap section, and state the limits plainly. Guna scores describe tendencies. They are not a diagnosis and should not be used to label anyone.

Two-sided test, 5% significance, 80% power, Fisher z method. With about 50 people per group, only fairly strong links (around r = .40 or more) can be found reliably, so a class survey is best described as a pilot study.

Limits

  • Self-report. People rate themselves, so answers can reflect what they want to be seen as, not what they are like.
  • Sattva is treated as best. Gunas carry a value judgement that the Big Five does not, which makes them less neutral for uses such as hiring.
  • Mostly Indian samples. Whether the Gunas work as a universal model or one rooted in Indian culture is unresolved.
  • The main source is exploratory. Srinivasan and Aithal (2026) say they collected information through Google, Google Scholar and AI-driven GPTs, and the Big Five match in their section 10.1 is not tied to specific studies. Use it to frame questions, and use the empirical papers for data.
  • Partial reading. Several studies here were read as abstracts only, as marked in the sources.
  • Not a systematic review. The gap is "not found in this search", not "proved absent".
